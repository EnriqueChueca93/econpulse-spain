About Macroscope Spain

A running read on the health of the Spanish economy: {{ total }} official series, updated together, shown as tables and charts with the method behind every number.

What this is

Macroscope Spain follows the idea of Econvitals, which does the same for the United States, and is not affiliated with it. Everything here comes from official or public sources, accessed through their open data services, and is rebuilt as a plain website that works offline.

The site has no forecasts of its own beyond those clearly labelled on the Analysis page, and nothing on it is investment advice.

How the numbers are made

As published, unless marked

Figures are shown as each source publishes them. Where the site changes or combines them, it says so: SA marks seasonal adjustment done here, and "Macroscope calculation" marks anything computed here.

Changes and growth rates

"y/y" compares with the same period a year earlier, "q/q" and "m/m" with the previous quarter or month. Following Spanish practice, quarterly GDP growth is not annualised. Tables comparing horizons annualise changes over less than a year, so that a 1-month and a 12-month rate can be read side by side. Rates move in basis points (bp, hundredths of a percentage point) or percentage points (pp); prices move in %.

Seasonal adjustment

Spain's CPI, the Social Security registrations and several other monthly series are published unadjusted. Where a short-term change needs adjusting, Macroscope uses STL (seasonal-trend decomposition with LOESS) on the logarithm of the series from 2010, with robust weights so that 2020 does not distort it, and seasonal factors that change only slowly. Results can differ from adjusted figures the source publishes.

Flash estimates and revisions

INE's flash CPI estimate appears as soon as it is published and is labelled as such. Every update downloads recent history again, so revisions replace earlier figures.

Freshness

Each series is checked against a delay that suits how often it is published. A series whose last download failed keeps its previous data and is marked "stale"; one whose source has stopped publishing is marked "not updating". Dates and times are Madrid time.

Macroscope originals

The composites, data surprises, GDP nowcast, forecasters' table, 10-year yield decomposition and Taylor rules are explained, with their assumptions and track record, on the Analysis page. ECB-speak scores the Governing Council's speeches with published word lists, not an AI, and shows how well the result tracks the ECB's decisions. The Fiscal Explorer starts from the European Commission's Debt Sustainability Monitor baseline for Spain, reproduced exactly to 2036, and extends it on the Commission's long-term assumptions; its method is explained on the page. The Global central bank stance page shows Econvitals' Global Central Bank Stance Matrix (econvitals.org, by Jason Furman), whose own work is in the public domain (CC0 1.0); it is refreshed daily from Econvitals' public data and credited on the page. Underlying inflation (weighted median and trimmed means) is computed from Eurostat's detailed HICP items and their yearly weights, following the ECB's method, and explained on the Inflation page. Participation and employment at the 2008 age mix, on the Labour market page, recombine the rates of men and women in 13 age groups from Eurostat's labour force survey with each group's 2008 share of the population. Collective dismissals and temporary layoffs (ERE and ERTE) come from the Ministry of Labour's monthly Regulación de Empleo workbooks, read from 2023. The Trade page sums Eurostat Comext's exports and imports by chapter of the Harmonised System into 18 product groups and reads its totals by partner; Comext follows the EU's community concept, so its totals differ slightly from Spain's customs figures. Its services by type come from Eurostat's quarterly balance of payments, and its export and import prices from Eurostat's short-term business statistics. The ECB page's market-implied path uses AAA government bond forward rates as a stand-in for swap rates, which are not freely available.

IMF-style sector tables

The sector tables follow the tables of the IMF's 2026 Article IV staff report for Spain line by line where public data allow. Actual years come from INE, Eurostat and the ECB and projection years from the IMF's World Economic Outlook database, which publishes only some of the report's lines; the others show "…", the IMF's mark for data that are not available. Potential output, the output gap and the structural balance are IMF staff estimates in every year. Where a line is derived, it follows the IMF's definition: the primary balance excludes interest received as well as interest paid, and public savings are the government balance plus public investment. The debt sustainability analysis splits the change in the debt ratio as the IMF does (primary deficit, the real interest rate, real growth, other flows); its stress tests come from the European Commission's Debt Sustainability Monitor, because the IMF's are not published as data. External debt is rebuilt from Eurostat's international investment position (liabilities in debt instruments), where the IMF uses the World Bank's external debt statistics. Banks' consolidated soundness indicators are computed from the IMF's own Financial Soundness Indicators database and their domestic exposures from the Banco de España's statistical bulletin. The monetary table has no counterpart in the report, since the ECB sets policy for the euro area. On the Sector tables page, tables with quarterly or monthly data also come by quarter or month (actual data only): flows as shares of that period's GDP, stocks as shares of GDP over the past four quarters. Past years can differ from the report's because the data have been revised since. All the tables, from 2015, are in one Excel workbook.

What changed and the calendar

After each update the site compares every series with the previous update and writes a short note for each new release, revision or large market move; the same notes make up the RSS feed. Revisions are noted only when the figure changes as shown. The release calendar uses INE's published calendar, the Social Security's usual publication day and the ECB's meeting schedule.

Charts

Charts are drawn in the browser with plotly.js (MIT licence), in its basic edition, which is all the site needs; it is served with the site, so nothing loads from elsewhere.

Limits

  • Data appear when the site is next updated, not the moment a source publishes them.
  • Some series have no clean free source yet, such as euro-area corporate credit spreads.
  • Several forecasters publish web pages and PDFs rather than data, so their figures are read from those pages; each new edition counts only after an AI check against the publisher's own document, and until then it is marked "awaiting AI check". The government's and AIReF's forecasts arrive through the FUNCAS Panel, up to two months after they are published.
  • Data surprises are measured against a simple statistical forecast, not against analysts' expectations, which are not freely available for Spanish releases.
  • Calculations are checked, but mistakes are possible: the source's own release is always the reference.

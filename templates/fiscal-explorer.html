How it works

The baseline is the Commission's. To {{ fx.last_commission_year }} the page shows the European Commission's baseline debt projection for Spain from its Debt Sustainability Monitor 2025 (February 2026), with its primary balance, interest bill and the growth, inflation and stock-flow effects behind each year's change in debt. Nothing is modelled: run through the debt equation, these reproduce the Commission's debt path to two decimals in every year. Like the Commission's, it is a no-policy-change baseline: from 2026 the structural primary balance stays put, except that the rising cost of ageing is paid for out of it.

After {{ fx.last_commission_year }}, Macroscope extends it on the Commission's own long-term assumptions from the 2024 Ageing Report: the primary balance falls by the further rise in pensions, health care, long-term care and education spending; real growth is the report's potential growth, plus 2% inflation; and the interest rate on the debt moves an eighth of the way each year toward the report's long-term market rate (about 4%), as maturing debt is refinanced. As a check, the adjustment this model needs to bring debt to 60% of GDP by 2070 is {{ "%.2f" | format(s1_check) }}% of GDP, against the Commission's {{ "%.2f" | format(fx.gaps.S1) }}% (its S1 indicator).

Levers. The budget lever changes the primary balance permanently. The ageing lever scales the projected rise in ageing costs after 2026 (100% is the Commission's; 0% freezes them at their 2026 share of GDP). Growth and inflation change nominal GDP; inflation and the interest-rate lever reach the interest bill only as debt is refinanced, an eighth a year. Revenue and spending are otherwise assumed to move with GDP. Higher debt does not, in this model, raise interest rates by itself, and the economy does not respond to the budget: both would make large adjustments look somewhat easier or harder than they are.
